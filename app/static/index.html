    Disco Diffusion Nano
    
        
    Disco Diffusion for 8GB GPUs, as a local app. Free and open source.

          neuromind.art
          Ko-fi
        
    
      
    
    
    
    
    Prompts
    
    
    Write one prompt per line, and all of them guide the image together. End a line with a weight to change how much it counts: :2 counts double, and :-1 steers away from it.
    
    
    Regional prompts
    
    
    The Regional prompts card puts boxes on the frame, each with a prompt of its own. Turn it on and press Edit regions, then drag on the frame to draw a box, type its prompt and press Done. Drag a box to move it, drag its corner to resize it, and press × to remove it. The boxes go with every render while the card is on, and turning it off keeps them for later. During a render the button reads Pause and edit regions: it pauses the render after the current step and opens the editor on the preview, and Continue carries on with the new boxes from there, as often as you like. A close-up inside a box is judged mostly by the box's prompt, and the main prompt keeps the rest, so the style stays the same. Strength sets how much a box's prompt takes over inside it. With Tiles, the boxes lie over the whole tiled picture.
    
    
    Fix
    
    
    Press Fix beside Stop while a render runs and it pauses after the current step. Pick Erase, Clone or Inpaint, paint on the preview, then press Apply and continue. You can use all three in one pause. Fix works with Tiles, and is for stills.
    
    
    Erase removes what you paint over. LaMa fills the area from what is around it, and Disco repaints it over the steps left, so there's no seam. If it grows back, erase again later, or raise Noise beside Brush: it starts at the share of fresh noise that suits the step, and more keeps things from coming back, but too much late on leaves grain. Erase once you can see the thing, from about a third of the way through: before that, the change spreads to the rest of the picture. Past 80%, erased areas come out soft, as the steps left can't add detail to them.
    
    
    Inpaint has a model of your own paint something into the area: choose it under Inpaint in Settings. Say what in the box beside it, or leave it empty for whatever fits around it. Fresh paints the area anew, Rework paints over what's there to change it, and Over a cleared area has LaMa clear it first. Strength sets how much Rework and Over a cleared area repaint. During a render, Inpaint paints a few tries first: the first shows on the picture, click any to see it in place, then Use this and continue. A model made for inpainting, such as Flux Fill, blends in best, and is listed first.
    
    
    Clone copies one part of the picture over another, at any step. Alt-click where to copy from, then paint: the source follows the brush, and Alt-click again for a new one. The copy's colors are matched to where it lands, and Disco carries on painting it. Late in a render, Clone and Inpaint keep detail where Erase can't.
    
    
    Something in the start image fixes best there, before the render. Press the brush on the start image, erase, inpaint or clone, and press Apply. The result is saved as a new image that replaces the start image, so the original is kept. The [ and ] keys and the mouse wheel change the brush size, E, C and I switch tools, and Ctrl+Z undoes a stroke.
    
    
    Looks
    
    
    A look sets how the prompt shapes the picture, at any size. Changing one of its settings by hand shows Custom.
    
    
    
    
    Cutouts and schedules
    
    
    
    
    
    
    The bar at the top of the card counts which steps are skipped, which a Handoff checkpoint paints, which lay out shapes and which paint detail, for the steps, Denoise or Frame skip and schedules you have set, and each schedule's graph marks where shapes end. A step lays out shapes while Whole-image views at least match Close-up views, so editing those two moves the split. The schedules run over 1000 points from noise to the finished picture. The first 400 lay out the scene with whole-image views and wide close-ups, and the rest paint detail with tighter and tighter close-ups. A start image skips the first steps, so at Denoise 50% Disco starts at point 500 and paints only detail over the image's shapes, which suits a restyle. Video frames do the same with Frame skip %. Fit views to the painted steps spreads the schedules over only the steps that are painted, so they start with a pass of shapes of their own and the prompt can turn what is in the image into what it describes. It's on for start images by default, and Start images and clip frames fits each frame of a clip too. Shapes share % sets how many of the steps that pass gets: 40 keeps the schedules as drawn, and 20 gives a fifth to shapes and the rest to detail.
    
    
    Settings that change over the render are schedules. Each one shows as a graph from the start of the render on the left to the end on the right, with its values in the corner. Click a graph to open its editor. Drag a level up or down to change it, and drag the line between two levels to move the change. Double-click a level to split it there, and double-click a line to join its two sides. The text box takes the same schedule as text, such as [12]*400+[4]*600: 12 for the first 400 of 1000 parts, then 4. A value that differs from the default shows in the accent color. Hover over a setting's name to read what it does.
    
    
    Guidance holds how the push toward the prompt is smoothed and limited, and how the picture is sampled. An option with settings of its own becomes a section when you switch it on, with its settings right under it, and its arrow folds them away. Settings that are always in use, such as those for Frames per second, start folded behind an arrow. A dot on an arrow means a setting inside is changed.
    
    
    Seeds and Exact repeat
    
    
    Seed 0 picks a new random seed for every render. Any other number starts from the same noise each time; slide it, or type any number up to 4294967295. A seed sets the starting noise, but the GPU adds tiny differences, so the same seed normally gives a similar start and a different image. Exact repeat removes those differences on this PC, at about 30% more time per step. Each image in a run has its own seed, so Reuse settings can make any of them again.
    
    
    Images
    
    
    Right-click the large image or any thumbnail to open it full size, copy it, save it, use it as a start image, reuse its settings, upscale it, or open its folder.
    
    
    Start images
    
    
    Drop an image on the box under the prompt, or click it to choose one. Sizing decides how the image meets the frame. Match sets the frame to the image's shape, at about the same size. Fit keeps the whole image and fills spare space with a blurred copy of it. Fill stretches the image to the frame. Crop fills the frame without stretching and cuts off what doesn't fit.
    
    
    
    
    
    
    Memory and CPU
    
    
    CPU threads in Settings limits how much of the CPU a render uses. More threads rarely make it faster, and the default of 4 keeps the PC responsive. Renders also run at below normal priority, so the desktop comes first.
    
    
    The bars at the bottom read the same way. Gray on the left is what the rest of the PC uses. Color on the right of it is Disco: green on VRAM, blue on RAM, purple for its files on the disk. Before a render, the colored part is lighter and shows what the next render will take, so it grows as you add to it. While a render runs, it turns solid and shows what Disco really uses. Hover any part to see its size.
    
    
    The striped end of VRAM is kept free so Windows can draw the screen. When a render won't fit in what is left, part of its working data moves to system RAM by itself, which is slower but works: that part leaves the green VRAM bar and shows green inside the RAM bar. Red means it's too big even then, so lower the size or use fewer prompt checkers. The secondary model is faster and lighter, at lower quality.
    
    
    Guide images
    
    
    Each box takes an image dropped on it, or click it to choose one. A ghost image guides the layout and a style image the texture and palette; their strengths are under Ghost settings and Style image settings in Guide images.
    
    
    Image prompts take many images at once: drop a set of images or a whole folder. They guide the render beside your text. Every prompt checker reads each image, and what the images have in common counts: a subject, a style, a palette, a mood. It's close to what an embedding does, but nothing is trained, so it takes seconds and works with any set. Ten to fifty images that share one idea work best. Image prompt weight sets how much they count beside a text line weighted 1, and below 0 steers away from them.
    
    
    Tiles
    
    
    Tiles builds a larger image from several renders at the frame size, so it needs no more VRAM than the frame does. Every tile takes each step in turn, and the overlaps are averaged before the next step, so neighbors settle on one image with no joins. Time grows with the number of tiles: 4 tiles take four times as long per step, 16 take sixteen times.
    
    
    
    
    
    
    Animation
    
    
    Animation renders a clip one frame at a time. 2D moves a flat picture each frame. 3D flies a camera through the scene, with Depth Anything telling near from far, so 3D needs it from Models. Video Input repaints each frame of a video you drop on its box. Every frame after the first starts from the last one, and Frame skip % sets how much of it is kept. Higher is steadier and faster. 75 holds a scene while the camera moves, and 60 lets it keep reinventing itself. Video Input switches to 55, so the video gets repainted more. Frame skip keyframes, under the arrow beside Frame skip %, can lower it around a prompt change, so the new prompt takes over sooner.
    
    
    Clip style, under the video, sets how each frame starts. Paint over keeps each frame's shapes from the video and paints over them, at Frame skip 40% with no blend, so its painted steps all paint detail: abstract and painterly, with Disco's flicker. Reimagine fits the views to each frame too, so its painted steps get the Look's own split of shapes and detail, and the prompt can turn what is in the video into what it describes. Build up keeps more of each frame and blends in some of the last one, so the painting builds up and changes less from frame to frame. Changing one of its settings by hand shows Custom.
    
    
    For calmer, more painterly clips, try Step noise at about 0.3, under the arrow beside Sampler in the Guidance card. It changes the first frame too.
    
    
    Each move has a slider for a steady value, and keyframes for change over time: 0:(1.0), 60:(1.04), 120:(1.0) eases in and out over 120 frames. Formulas of the frame t work too: 0:(1+0.02*sin(t/8)) sways. The curve button opens an editor: drag a point to change it, double-click empty space to add a keyframe, and double-click a point to remove it. Formulas typed in its text box show as hollow points that can't be dragged.
    
    
    The prompt box is the prompt at the first frame. Change the prompt at a frame adds another box with the frame it starts at, so a clip can travel from one idea to the next. Prompt fade, under the arrow beside Change the prompt at a frame, blends one into the next.
    
    
    Hold colors keeps the light and color close to the first frame's, so long clips don't drift. Turbo repaints only every few frames and lets the camera move the frames between, which is much faster. Last frame as ghost lets Depth target and the ghost's light and dark hold each frame to the last, which is steadier. For Video Input, Blend last frame mixes the last finished frame into each new one, so the painting builds up over the clip, and it follows the video's own motion, so the paint moves with what moves. The first frame gets a first pass of its own, so the clip doesn't open on the plain video.
    
    
    Seamless loop makes a clip that loops with no jump where it starts again. Paint back to the start paints the last few frames more and more like the first, for motion that already comes back to where it began, such as a camera moving on a sin formula. Ping-pong plays the video, or the camera's moves, forward and then back, so the motion ends where it began, and paints back to the start as well. The way back is painted fresh, not played in reverse. For a video, Frames counts both ways. With In-between frames, the last frame gets in-betweens into the first too.
    
    
    Each clip is saved as PNG frames in a folder, an MP4 and an animated WebP, with a GIF if you choose. Stopping a clip still saves the video of the frames it has. The last frame shows in Images, and clicking it plays the clip.
    
    
    Handoff
    
    
    Handoff brings in a model you already have, SD 1.5, SDXL, Z-Image, Flux.1 or Flux.2 Klein: it paints first and Disco repaints its picture, or it finishes Disco's image, or both in one render.
    
    
    The checkpoint's settings come first once a checkpoint is chosen, and both ways use them: its steps, CFG, sampler, Karras schedule, Clip skip, few-step spacing and negative prompt. Each checkpoint remembers its own, so choosing it again brings them back.
    
    
    Checkpoint, then Disco. Handoff % is how much of its picture the checkpoint paints. At 100, the default, it finishes its picture, and Disco repaints Denoise of it, in the Render card, as it would a start image, so the layout and subject come from the checkpoint and the paint from Disco. Choosing a checkpoint sets Denoise to 60% if it was at 100%. Below 100, the checkpoint hands over a picture still forming and Disco carries on from that point, so Denoise isn't used; about 30% keeps Disco's look on a sturdier layout. 0 turns this part off. With a start image, or on a clip's frames, the checkpoint starts from it, and Start denoise % is how much of it the checkpoint repaints: lower lets more of your image through, and 0 uses the same share as Denoise.
    
    
    Disco, then checkpoint. Finish % is the reverse handoff: after Disco is done, the checkpoint repaints that much of the finished image, like Denoise. 0 is off, 20 to 35% tidies details such as faces and edges and keeps Disco's look, and 100% replaces Disco's image. It works with Handoff % at 0, for still images only. Save its picture also saves Disco's own image beside the result.
    
    
    Small first, in the Size card, hands Disco's own start from a small size to the full size, and needs no checkpoint. Disco's model learned at about 512 pixels, and its prompt checkers look at small views of the picture, so on a large frame such as 1280×768 different areas can each grow their own subject. Small first paints the start of each image at a smaller size in the frame's shape, while the layout is decided, then enlarges its still blurry picture and carries on at full size, where the detail is painted. Small first % is the small share: 0 is off and about 25% suits 1280×768. Full-size denoise % works like Denoise: 0 carries on from exactly where the small start stopped, and higher has the full size repaint more of it, which gives it more say over the layout. Hold the small layout, also in that section, keeps the full size's broad shapes and colors close to the small picture while the detail is added, so a second subject can't grow in the empty areas; about 0.5 is a good start. Small first size is the small start's longer side, 768 by default, and Small first does nothing on frames no larger than that. The small steps are faster, so a render also takes a little less time. It works on stills, and on any frame of a clip whose skipped steps leave some of its small share to paint, but not with Tiles, which have their own Render first, or with the experiments that are sized to the frame. It's off while a Handoff checkpoint is chosen, which already paints the start at its own size.
    
    
    The checkpoint waits in RAM and comes to the GPU only for its own steps. SD 1.5 fits beside Disco. SDXL is stored in 8 bits on cards under 12GB, which halves its memory, and takes a minute or two to load the first time; after that it stays loaded between renders. Z-Image, Flux and Klein, as .safetensors or .gguf, stream from RAM a block at a time when they don't fit.
    
    
    Z-Image and Flux come as separate files, as in ComfyUI: Z-Image needs its Qwen3-4B text encoder, Flux needs T5-XXL and CLIP-L, both with Flux's autoencoder, ae.safetensors. Klein 4B needs Qwen3-4B and Klein 9B Qwen3-8B, both with flux2-vae.safetensors. Nano finds them in the models folder the model is in, subfolders included. Choosing one sets the steps and CFG it was made for, and the settings it doesn't use gray out.
    
    
    The Handoff list shows the SD 1.5 and SDXL checkpoints in your checkpoint folders on the PC that renders, and the Z-Image, Flux and Flux.2 Klein models in the unet and diffusion_models folders beside them, grouped by subfolder. The welcome screen fills in the folders of ComfyUI or a Stable Diffusion web UI it finds, and Find in other apps, beside Checkpoint folders in Settings, looks again. Browse picks a file anywhere on the PC that renders and adds its folder.
    
    
    Controls
    
    
    ControlNet's idea, done through guidance, since Disco's model has nowhere to plug a ControlNet in. At every step the render is compared with a control picture and nudged closer, beside the prompt. A still follows the Control image, or else its start image. A clip needs none: a Video Input clip follows each frame of the video as it is before any painting, so the subject stays where it really is, and a 2D or 3D clip follows the frame each repaint starts from. Edge control holds its outlines, and only asks for an outline where it has one. Depth control holds its near and far. Pose control finds each person's skeleton and holds a body in that stance. Person control cuts out each person and holds their silhouette, clothes and broad colors, while everything around them is painted freely; it is the strongest for keeping someone in a clip. Face control finds each face and holds its features while the layout forms, where the eyes, nose and mouth are and their light and dark, then lets the paint finish them; a head turned to the side can still be painted facing forward; Face shape holds more of a turn. Face shape holds each face's outline and its near and far, measured close up, so the face keeps its own shape and a turned head keeps more of its turn, most of all with a Control image; it pushes beside the prompt as Edge and Depth do, so the face is still painted freely. Face detail gives every face close-ups of its own at each step, so faces are painted with more care; with no control picture it finds faces in the render itself, so it works with text alone. About 0.5 is a good start, and Person control holds well at 0.3. Each one has its own curve, in its section, which sets when it counts: Face lets go at 50% of the render, Depth at 60%, Pose, Person and Face shape at 70%, and Edge at 80%. They don't work with Tiles.
    
    
    Experiments
    
    
    These change how the image forms. They're new, so results vary a lot from seed to seed, and they can't run with tiles, symmetry, plms or the secondary model. Whichever you choose first grays out the other, and hovering a grayed setting says why.
    
    
    Time travel backs up and repeats some steps, so the painter can absorb the prompt's push. Subjects get cleaner, for about 35% more time.
    
    
    Four experiments use a small text model, the partner, beside Disco. Get Tiny SD from Models in Settings, about 900MB.
    
    
    Prompt graft adds the effect of your prompt straight into Disco's own decoder, which normally can't read text. Subjects often come out whole, and Disco's paint and color stay. About 3 is a good start. It adds about 0.6GB of VRAM and works only with the 512x512 model.
    
    
    Live ghost lets the partner guide the picture's broad light and dark, and leaves color and detail to Disco. Sketch first paints a quick sketch before the render and takes it out of memory again, so it needs no extra VRAM during the render. Live keeps repainting during the render.
    
    
    Depth target keeps near and far where the ghost has them. A small depth model reads the ghost once and then checks the render at every step, so it works with Live ghost or a Ghost image. About 1 is a good start, and it pairs well with Sketch first and Prompt graft. It adds about 0.1 to 0.25GB of VRAM and a few seconds. Get Depth Anything from Models in Settings, about 100MB.
    
    
    Two painters shares every step between the two models. The partner owns the broad shapes until the layout is set, at 40%, and Disco owns detail and finishes alone. Holding the shapes longer, in Two painters curve, flattens the light and dark.
    
    
    Borrowed attention gives Disco the partner's sense of which parts of the picture belong together. Subjects often improve. Borrowed layers sets where: Coarse borrows only the broad layout and keeps Disco's color, All borrows everywhere for a stronger, grayer result, and Coarsest changes the least. It works especially well with Time travel.
    
    
    Five more change the checkers themselves, the CLIP models that compare the picture with your prompt. A checker is as happy with bits of a subject scattered over the canvas as with one whole subject, which is why Disco subjects fall apart. None of these needs the partner, and they can be combined.
    
    
    Coherence critic shows the checkers each view twice, once with its pieces shuffled, and pushes the picture so the shuffled one scores worse. Your prompt then only counts when it comes from how the parts fit together, so subjects hold together and the paint stays. About 0.6 is a good start. It adds about 10% time and about 0.9GB of VRAM, or 0.65GB with Coherence views set to Whole-image.
    
    
    Robust checker adds a checker that was trained to resist tiny changes, so its push draws real shapes instead of streaks. It leads for the first third of the render, while the layout forms, then hands over to the usual checkers. Expect whole scenes and steadier subjects, with more muted color. FARE4 B/16 costs almost nothing; the L/14 ones are about 70% slower and bolder. Get them from Models in Settings. Robust checker mode set to Together keeps the usual checkers at full strength all along.
    
    
    One subject finds where each subject sits in the picture and pushes it into one place, so it isn't repeated, and keeps separate subjects apart. Name them in Subjects, in the One subject section once it's on, separated by semicolons, such as "a woman; a white horse". Left empty, it uses the first phrase of your prompt. About 1 suits one subject and 2 suits two; higher can lose a face. It needs a ViT checker.
    
    
    Corrector steps add small extra steps early in the render, which let the picture settle at each stage before it moves on. One step gives cleaner faces and steadier layouts for about 50% more time. With Two painters, they also make both models agree, which changes Two painters a lot.
    
    
    Prompt routing scores the subject of your prompt on whole-image views and the rest, its style, on close-ups, so the subject forms once and the paint covers everything. The subject is the first phrase of each line, up to the first comma, or the first line. Its curve applies it for the first half of the render by default; the whole render can turn abstract. It costs nothing.
    
    
    Push momentum goes wilder and more abstract as it rises. Broad shapes first, Subject memory and Shaped prompt strength give smaller changes.
    
    
    Models
    
    
    Setup downloads the main model. The others are optional and grayed out in the Model list until you download them from Models in Settings. All of them were tested at 512×512. The style models use about 2GB less VRAM than the main model and take a little over half the time.
    
    
    Rendering on another PC
    
    
    To render on a PC with a GPU while you work on another, turn on Share this PC in Settings on the PC with the GPU. It shows an address and a code. On the other PC, open the address in any browser, or type it into Render on another PC in Nano's own Settings, and type the code once. That PC then uses Nano as if it sat at the GPU PC, and renders, images and settings all live there. From the other PC, Browse and Add folder show the GPU PC's own drives, so handoff checkpoints, the partner's SD 1.5 file and upscaler folders are picked from what is on the GPU PC. Start images, videos and guide images are dropped onto the page and uploaded. Where images are saved, and opening Explorer, are set on the GPU PC itself. Windows may ask once whether Python can use private networks: choose Allow. A new code signs out every other PC.
    
    
    Folders
    
    
    Settings sets where images are saved and where to look for upscalers and checkpoints. You can download Real-ESRGAN x4plus from Models in Settings, or point Nano at a folder of your own.
    
    
    
    
    Every setting
    
    
    Setting names in Settings switches every name here between plain and official. Lowest and Highest are the ends of each setting's useful range. Going past them is likely to waste time or fail. Schedule settings give the value for one step, written over a 1000-step run: [12]*400+[4]*600 means 12 for the first 400 and 4 for the last 600.
